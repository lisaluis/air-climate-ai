Emissions barely changed.
The air flipped.

In winter 2022–23, north India breathed up to cleaner air than usual while Mumbai’s PM2.5 rose . A year later the two swapped places. The difference was the weather, not the chimneys.

Same smoke, different wind

El Niño and La Niña are the two phases of ENSO, a natural cycle in the Pacific Ocean. Each rearranges the winter winds over India. The study found that this changed where PM2.5 was carried and where it was trapped.

La Niña, 2022–23

Stronger winds over the north dispersed smoke from stubble burning and local sources. The circulation bypassed Delhi and carried pollutants south to cities such as Mumbai and Thiruvananthapuram. Rain also helped: rainfall was more closely tied to lower PM2.5 that winter.

El Niño, 2023–24

Winds over the north were weaker and moved through Delhi and Jaipur instead of carrying pollution away. High pressure, weak winds and a shallow boundary layer, a thin layer of air near the ground, kept PM2.5 close to the surface. Backward air-mass tracing showed Jaipur kept receiving polluted northern air, while Hyderabad received cleaner air from the sea and the south.

Year to year, PM2.5 moved from about to against the 2019–22 average with no major change in emissions or pollution-control policy.

What the study does not say

The authors stress that the link is correlation, not proven cause, and cannot be applied to other years. El Niño does not always worsen air in Delhi. Only two unusual winters were studied, and the authors do not imply the 2023–24 pattern will repeat this winter.

The climate map is moving too

A separate study by IMD and the Indian Institute of Tropical Meteorology compared India’s climate map decade by decade from 1951 to 2020. In the northwest the hot, dry zone has grown and pushed east into Uttar Pradesh, one of the country’s most important farming regions. In the peninsula, rising rainfall moved most of the old hot semi-arid land into the wetter tropical savannah zone, which now covers about three-quarters of the region.

What policy can do when weather decides

If weather can swing PM2.5 by tens of percent, cutting local emissions is necessary but not enough to promise a clean winter. These three options are proposals this project explores. They are not conclusions of the studies.
